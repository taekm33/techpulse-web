On September 12, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published an essay on his personal site titled "We Must Pace the Frontier." He warned that while AI can deliver enormous benefits, the capabilities of "agents" — systems that can write their own code, chain tools together, and operate with limited human oversight — are advancing faster than the industry's ability to monitor them. Citing risks of losing control and of misuse in cyberattacks and bioterrorism, he argued that AI companies and governments must deliberately moderate the pace of capability gains.

## Amodei's three-step plan

The core of the essay is a three-step roadmap for pacing development. Notably, Amodei said Anthropic would implement the first step on its own rather than wait for other companies to join.

| Step | What it means |
|---|---|
| ① Embedded evaluators | Give third-party evaluators permanent, employee-level access to verify adherence to safety measures, report incidents, and assess model alignment. Anthropic commits first |
| ② Democratic coordination | Frontier AI firms in democratic countries jointly set common safety standards and limits on the rate of unchecked progress |
| ③ Global coordination | The US and other democratic governments attempt to coordinate even with authoritarian governments, while confronting the difficulty of verifying compliance |

## It dovetails with talks on an industry standards body

The essay is not an isolated opinion; it lines up with quiet, ongoing industry discussions. According to reports, Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google DeepMind have held working-group meetings since July on creating an industry-led AI safety standards body. Google DeepMind CEO Demis Hassabis proposed a "Frontier AI Standards Body" modeled on the US Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA) on July 14 — a body that would assess advanced models before release.

The three companies differ, however, on how much government involvement is appropriate. Anthropic leans toward partnership with government, while OpenAI emphasizes voluntary industry standards.

<div class="article-callout info">
FINRA is a US self-regulatory model in which the securities industry oversees its own member firms. Hassabis's proposal follows the logic of "set voluntary standards ourselves rather than wait for government regulation," overlapping in direction with Amodei's steps 2 and 3.
</div>

## Trump's flat rejection

The political reaction split sharply. Speaking to reporters at his Doonbeg golf resort in Ireland on September 14, President Donald Trump dismissed warnings about AI as exaggerated and waved off the calls to slow down. "We're leading China in AI, we're the most sophisticated country in the world, and frankly, I want to keep it that way — because whoever wins AI, wins," he said, and he criticized Amodei by name.

In a Monday social media post, Trump characterized the public backlash over AI data centers and concern about frontier models as a "SICK conspiracy," adding that "the only one that is happy about it is China."

<div class="article-callout tip">
Once safety becomes a political flashpoint, companies' voluntary commitments are exposed to regulatory uncertainty. Organizations weighing AI adoption are better served checking whether structural safeguards — third-party verification, auditability — actually function, rather than taking any single vendor's "voluntary safety pledge" at face value.
</div>

## What it means and what's next

The episode shows the center of gravity in the AI race shifting from "who ships a stronger model fastest" toward "how fast a pace society can absorb." While the frontier labs move toward voluntary pacing and self-governance, the White House is skeptical of regulation itself — leaving a clear gap between "industry self-rule" and "state-led oversight." Whether Anthropic's unilateral first step draws real participation from rivals, or remains a symbolic gesture, is the thing to watch next.
