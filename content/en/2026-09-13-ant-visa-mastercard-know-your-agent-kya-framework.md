On September 9, Ant International, Mastercard and Visa announced they will collaborate on a "Know Your Agent" (KYA) interoperability framework to verify and identify AI agents across payment networks. Each already runs its own protocol — Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol (TAP), Mastercard's Verifiable Intent, and Ant's Agentic Mobile Protocol (AMP) — and the plan is to connect them under shared principles so trust signals can be mutually recognized. As AI agents move from recommending purchases to completing them on a user's behalf, network-level identity and accountability is emerging as core infrastructure.
</div>

As "agentic commerce" — AI that picks items and pays on a user's behalf — moves from concept to reality, the payment industry's biggest players are starting to align on how those agents get verified. Ant International, Mastercard and Visa said on September 9 that they have begun developing a "Know Your Agent" (KYA) interoperability framework to standardize AI-agent onboarding and identification across payment networks.

## What they agreed on

The heart of the initiative is interoperability. All three firms already operate their own protocols for verifying AI agents: Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol (TAP), Mastercard's Verifiable Intent, and Ant International's Agentic Mobile Protocol (AMP). The KYA framework does not replace those standards; instead, it aims to establish common principles so each network can recognize the others' trust signals.

Done well, this spares card networks, digital wallet ecosystems, agent platforms and marketplaces from onboarding and verifying every agent separately. When common trust signals travel across the payment ecosystem, the companies argue, duplicative verification and integration complexity fall, while new agentic services reach market faster and at lower integration cost.

## Why now — the rise of agent-driven payments

The backdrop is an eye-catching market forecast. The announcement said AI agents "are evolving from making recommendations to completing purchases on behalf of users," and that "by 2030, AI agents are projected to orchestrate $3 trillion to $5 trillion of global consumer commerce."

The catch is trust. If networks cannot verify whether an agent is genuinely authorized to act for a user, what actions it may take, and who is accountable when something goes wrong, the risk of fraud and malfunction can ripple across the entire payment system. That is precisely where the KYA architecture comes in — establishing requirements and accountability, verifying agent identities, and ensuring secure, trusted transaction execution.

<div class="article-callout tip">
For any service or marketplace looking to plug in agents, designing to a common framework like KYA — rather than integrating each network's agent authentication separately — is likely to pay off in both time-to-market and integration cost.
</div>

## The cost of bot management

Signs that agent identity is shifting from "nice to have" to "must have" show up in the data. A PYMNTS Intelligence report published in March found that nearly 90% of enterprises say bot management has become a major challenge, and that outdated digital-identity controls cost businesses roughly $100 billion each year in fraud, false declines and lost customers.

Ant International Chief Innovation Officer Jiang-Ming Yang said in the release that the company looks forward to "expanding collaboration as the industry draws on richer signals — capabilities, behavior, execution performance and risk data — to enhance trust and simplify transactions for our clients and their customers."

## What it means

The collaboration underscores how the center of gravity in AI is shifting from raw model performance toward the infrastructure that lets agents actually spend money. That rivals Visa and Mastercard are aligning alongside Ant is itself a sign that agentic commerce has reached a point where no single company's standard can carry it. The hard part remains: making three distinct protocols genuinely interoperable is far tougher than agreeing on principles, and the outcome will hinge on the technical details and on how liability is divided.

<div class="article-callout info">
There is a wide gap between agreeing on an authentication standard and implementing one. A statement of common principles is only a starting point; agentic payments will scale only once networks settle how far they will honor one another's trust signals and who bears responsibility when a transaction goes wrong.
</div>
