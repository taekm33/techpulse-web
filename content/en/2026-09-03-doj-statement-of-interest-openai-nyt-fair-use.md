<div class="article-tldr">
The U.S. Department of Justice filed a statement of interest in the copyright suit The New York Times brought against OpenAI and Microsoft, formally backing OpenAI's argument that training large language models on copyrighted works qualifies as fair use. The government said a robust American AI ecosystem is essential to national security and economic priorities, and that the plaintiffs are reading fair use far too narrowly. It is the first time the U.S. government has weighed in directly on the wave of AI copyright litigation.
</div>

The U.S. Justice Department filed a statement of interest late Tuesday in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York, siding with OpenAI in the copyright infringement suits brought by The New York Times and other news outlets and authors. The government argued that training AI models on copyrighted works falls within fair use, and that the plaintiffs are defining the doctrine more narrowly than the law requires.

## What Was Filed

A statement of interest is a document a non-party government files to convey its view of the public interest to a court. This one was submitted before Judge Sidney Stein, who is overseeing the coordinated copyright cases. The DOJ argued that AI development is critical to national security, and that the training process sufficiently transforms the original works into new material permitted under copyright law.

## The Government's Argument

The DOJ's case runs along two lines. First, that LLM training is a transformative use—not a wholesale copy or substitute for the originals—and therefore a paradigm case of fair use. Second, that a ruling for the plaintiffs would harm U.S. technological innovation and national security.

In its filing, the department wrote that the "creative possibilities and public benefits" of training LLMs on such content "far outweigh any competitive harm." It also warned that constraining LLM development under a misunderstanding of fair use "would thwart ... creative and scientific progress while hindering American prosperity and economic mobility."

| Issue | Plaintiffs (publishers, authors) | Justice Department |
|---|---|---|
| Use of training data | Unauthorized copying = infringement | Transformative use = fair use |
| Scope of fair use | Read narrowly | Read broadly |
| Governing priority | Protecting creators' rights | Innovation and national security |

## Background to the Suit

The New York Times sued OpenAI and Microsoft in December 2023, alleging they used millions of its articles without permission to train AI models. Similar suits from other outlets and creators—The Intercept, the New York Daily News, the Center for Investigative Reporting, and author groups—have since been coordinated before Judge Stein in the Southern District. The government's intervention bears on that coordinated litigation as a whole.

<div class="article-callout info">
A statement of interest is a non-binding opinion, not a ruling. The government's position does not decide the case; whether the use is fair remains for Judge Stein and the court to determine.
</div>

## Why It Matters

The intervention signals that the administration has put its weight on the industry's side of the AI copyright debate. The legality of training data is a shared exposure for OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Meta alike, so the filing's reasoning could be cited across other suits. Publishers and creators, meanwhile, argue the government is favoring specific companies' business models—leaving the data practices of the entire U.S. AI industry riding on how the court rules.

<div class="article-callout tip">
Fair use is decided case by case, weighing four factors: the purpose and character of the use, the nature of the work, the amount used, and the effect on the market. A claim of "transformative use" alone does not settle the question.
</div>
