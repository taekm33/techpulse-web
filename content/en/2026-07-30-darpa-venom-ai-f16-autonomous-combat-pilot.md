## An AI Just Took the Stick on a Real Fighter Jet

From the outside, it looked like any other F-16 sortie rolling off the Eglin Air Force Base flightline in Florida. But on July 16, 2026, the cockpit told a different story: for the first time, an artificial intelligence agent controlled a standard, combat-configured F-16 Fighting Falcon in live flight.

DARPA's **VENOM (Viper Experimentation and Next-generation Operations Model)** program reached a landmark milestone when a test pilot took off in a VENOM-modified F-16, then handed autonomous control over to the AI onboard — and the AI flew the jet.

This is not a simulation. It's not a drone. It's a fully operational F-16, with a pilot in the seat watching as a machine learns to fight.

## What Is the VENOM Autonomy Kit?

The **VENOM Autonomy Kit (VAK)** is an aftermarket hardware, software, and instrumentation package that interfaces with an F-16's existing flight controls and mission systems — critically, **without modifying the jet's core software**. That design choice is deliberate: AI agents trained on VENOM jets operate under the exact same sensors, weapons models, flight dynamics, and communication systems as every other F-16 in the operational fleet.

A pilot can hand over — or reclaim — control with a single switch, making this a true "human-on-the-loop" system. The human is always present, can monitor AI decision-making in real time, and can intervene instantly. What's new is that the AI is genuinely flying the plane.

Physical additions to each aircraft include an **auto-throttle**, giving the AI direct authority over engine thrust. Flight control surfaces are handled through the existing Flight Control System computer. The onboard hardware ensures the AI cannot exceed the jet's flight envelope or safety limits — a hard mechanical guardrail around the AI's autonomy.

<div class="article-callout info">
<strong>Building on ACE:</strong> VENOM is the successor to DARPA's Air Combat Evolution (ACE) program, which proved an AI agent could dogfight against a human-piloted F-16 aboard the one-of-a-kind X-62 VISTA aircraft. VENOM scales that capability to standard operational jets — a much harder and more consequential test.

## The Road to Multi-Ship Combat AI

The first autonomous VENOM flight is the starting line, not the finish line. DARPA's broader **AIR (Artificial Intelligence Reinforcements)** program has a structured escalation path for the VENOM fleet:

| Phase | Scenario | Description |
|-------|----------|-------------|
| 1 | Basic fighter maneuvers | Close-range dogfighting |
| 2 | 1v1, within visual range | Short-range single combat |
| 3 | 1v1, beyond visual range (BVR) | Long-range missile engagements |
| 4 | 2v2 multi-ship | Coordinated wingman tactics |
| 5 | 4v4 multi-ship | Full squadron-level combat complexity |

Brig. Gen. James "Fangs" Valpiani, the DARPA program manager, noted that 4v4 scenarios "take perhaps one to two years to become proficient in as a new pilot" — the AI will have to earn its way through the same ladder.

The scenarios will also incorporate electronic warfare environments and simulated missile engagements, pushing the AI to handle the uncertainty, deception, and degradation that real pilots face.

<div class="article-callout tip">
<strong>Why standard jets matter:</strong> Testing AI on a specially modified one-of-a-kind aircraft (like the X-62 VISTA) proves a concept. Testing it on unmodified operational F-16s — with no software changes, real-world sensors, and actual mission systems — validates a deployable technology. The AI must work under exactly the conditions a warfighter would face, not idealized lab conditions.
</div>

## From VENOM to the Unmanned Future

The combat AI agents validated on VENOM jets are ultimately destined for the **Collaborative Combat Aircraft (CCA)** program — the U.S. Air Force's strategy for pairing crewed fighters with AI-controlled autonomous wingmen. Two CCA aircraft are currently in development: **General Atomics' FQ-42** and **Anduril Industries' FQ-44**.

The vision: a single human pilot commanding a heterogeneous team of crewed and autonomous aircraft, with AI handling the speed, precision, and endurance that exceed human limits — particularly in the beyond-visual-range domain where modern air combat increasingly plays out.

Terry Wilson, AIR's deputy program manager, described the aspiration plainly: "I look forward to unleashing the VENOM platform and moving these capabilities out of simulation and into the sky."

DARPA says the program will ultimately "pave the way for human pilots to seamlessly command and orchestrate teams of autonomous, uncrewed aircraft."
