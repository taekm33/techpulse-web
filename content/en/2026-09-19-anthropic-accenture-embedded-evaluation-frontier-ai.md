Anthropic is opening its most advanced models to outside eyes. On September 18, the company said it is partnering with consulting giant Accenture on independent evaluation of frontier AI. This is not an ordinary audit contract: the weight of the announcement sits in "embedded evaluation," in which evaluators take up positions inside an AI company and look at the development process from within.

## What's different — from "inspecting the finished product" to "living inside development"

Until now, external evaluators have mostly been handed a completed model to test. Embedded evaluation flips that arrangement. Evaluators get access comparable to an employee's: they can watch a model take shape through training, follow the decisions that govern how it is built and deployed, and speak directly with employees. From that vantage point they can assess how the company actually operates, verify that it is keeping the safety commitments it has made, and identify blind spots. They can also report incidents and give the public a more accurate account of the benefits and risks.

Anthropic drew a clear line: "independent embedded evaluators do not reduce our accountability, but help to make it more verifiable." The safety of its models, in other words, remains Anthropic's own responsibility.

## Who does the work — Accenture's AI arm, Faculty

The day-to-day evaluation work is led by Faculty, Accenture's specialist AI business. The scope covers evaluating and red-teaming models (probing aggressively for weaknesses), conducting alignment assessments, and testing model safeguards. Accenture helps businesses and governments across many industries actually deploy AI, and Anthropic says that understanding of how AI is used in practice will inform the evaluation work.

## Who pays — Anthropic directly for now, public funding long-term

Embedded evaluation is new, and much of how it will operate is still undecided. There are no standards yet for what information evaluators should have access to or how they should report what they find, and no settled system for funding independent evaluation. Anthropic believes that, long-term, the money should come from pooled or government sources — a direction it already proposed in its Advanced AI Framework in June. Since no such funding exists today, Anthropic will fund Accenture's work directly for now.

<div class="article-callout tip">
Embedded evaluation directly targets a limit of traditional safety checks, where regulators or independent auditors see only the finished product after the fact. The catch: when the evaluator is paid by the company it evaluates (currently Anthropic pays directly), critics may question its independence. Whether Anthropic actually pushes for the shift to public funding and shared standards will decide how much trust this experiment earns.
</div>

## Not locked to one partner — a non-exclusive ecosystem

The partnership is non-exclusive. Anthropic plans to work with other evaluators to be announced in the coming weeks, and Accenture may work with other AI developers in similar capacities. Anthropic also said it is in dialogue with the nonprofit evaluator METR and other organizations about piloting elements of embedded evaluation using their own funding. Frontier AI, the company argued, needs "an ecosystem of evaluators operating with shared standards."

## What it means

The announcement is an attempt to pull frontier-AI safety verification out of the binary between internal self-governance and external after-the-fact auditing. Placing evaluators on the development floor creates room to catch problems as models form, not only afterward. Two questions will decide its value. First, independence: can evaluators reach uncomfortable conclusions while the company foots the bill? Second, standardization: shared rules for access and reporting must take hold before the approach can spread to other companies. The pledge Dario Amodei made under the banner of "pacing the frontier" is, with this $2 billion first deal, starting to take a verifiable shape.
