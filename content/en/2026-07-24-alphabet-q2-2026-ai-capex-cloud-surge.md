Alphabet, Google's parent company, reported Q2 2026 results after the bell on July 22. Total revenue came in at <strong>$119.8 billion, up 24% year over year</strong> (from $96.4B a year earlier), topping consensus. GAAP diluted EPS was $9.11, but that figure included a roughly $99 billion one-time gain on equity securities (contributing about $6.26 to EPS), so it does not cleanly reflect operating performance. On revenue alone, it was a clean surprise. Yet shares dropped roughly 4% in after-hours trading. What the market fixated on wasn't the revenue — it was the sheer scale of capital being poured in to produce it.

## The Real Star Was Cloud — Revenue +82%, Margin 35.6%

The quarter's true highlight wasn't search advertising but <strong>Google Cloud</strong>. Cloud revenue surged <strong>82% year over year to $24.8 billion</strong>, and operating margin expanded to <strong>35.6%</strong>. As important as the growth rate was the <strong>backlog</strong>: Cloud backlog swelled by more than $50 billion during the quarter to roughly <strong>$514 billion</strong>.

Why that matters: backlog is <strong>future revenue already under contract</strong>. If the growth rate reflects demand right now, a $514B backlog means quarters — and years — of cloud revenue are already booked. And to serve that demand, the data centers and chips have to physically exist. That's where the spending story begins.

## What Rattled the Market Was the Spending

The cause of the sell-off is unambiguous. Alphabet's Q2 <strong>capital expenditure hit $44.9 billion</strong>, a quarterly record and roughly double the year-ago figure. The bigger jolt was the <strong>annual guidance</strong>: the company lifted its 2026 capex outlook from the <strong>$180B–$190B</strong> it had given just one quarter earlier to <strong>$195B–$205B</strong> — pushing the top of its annual plan above $200 billion in a single step.

Spending at that pace pressures cash flow. Free cash flow turned negative this quarter under the weight of the record capex. CEO Sundar Pichai and management justified the build-out by arguing that AI demand keeps outrunning their ability to supply it — but the market split. The revenue and cloud growth are plainly strong; the worry is that the capital being burned right now to fund that growth is squeezing near-term profitability.

<div class="article-callout tip">
One lens: the real message here isn't "did Google do well" but rather <strong>"is AI demand real."</strong> A company raising the top of its annual capex to above $200B in a single quarter is telling you the contracted demand behind it — that $514B cloud backlog — is genuinely large. This is a signal backed by orders and contracts, not marketing. The market's actual question is simply <em>when</em> that bet turns into profit.
</div>

## Context — After TSMC and ASML, Another Layer of the AI Boom

This report sits on a continuum with the semiconductor and infrastructure earnings of recent weeks. <strong>ASML (Jul 15)</strong> announced a 30% capacity expansion and <strong>TSMC (Jul 16)</strong> posted record AI-driven revenue, confirming the ballast beneath the AI boom. Alphabet's results show the same picture one level up the pyramid — from the vantage point of a <strong>hyperscaler pouring money into infrastructure</strong>. When the equipment maker (ASML) and the foundry (TSMC) say orders are flooding in, companies like Alphabet are the ones placing those orders.

The quarter also distills the <strong>tension</strong> of the hyperscaler investment cycle. Capex on AI infrastructure is the precondition for future cloud revenue, but the payback timing is uncertain, and cash flow and margins take the strain in the meantime. That the stock fell despite a revenue beat signals that the market is now watching the growth rate <em>and</em> the bill for that growth in the same frame.
