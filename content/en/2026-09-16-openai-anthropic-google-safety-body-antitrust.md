The AI industry's top competitors joining forces on safety has now moved onto the public record. Chris Lehane, OpenAI's global policy chief, said at a briefing in Washington on September 15 that the company's engagement with Anthropic and Google DeepMind on AI safety had been under way for "several weeks." First reported by Bloomberg, his remarks were the first time the long-rumored, behind-the-scenes talks among the three labs were confirmed by a participant.

"It's better to try to work together to prioritize safety," Lehane said, adding that he does not believe the three firms need an antitrust waiver to coordinate on safety matters. The companies are discussing a new industry-wide body that would establish common standards for the world's most powerful artificial intelligence systems.

## Behind-the-scenes talks surface — Amodei's essay pulled the trigger

The backdrop to this public confirmation is an essay published on September 12 by Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, titled "We Must Pace the Frontier." According to reporting, the three firms had quietly been discussing an industry-led safety standards body since July 2026, and Amodei's essay pushed that conversation into public view.

In the essay, Amodei laid out a three-step framework. First, place employee-level third-party evaluators inside AI labs. Anthropic made a unilateral commitment here, pledging to give outside verifiers — including the AI safety assessment organization METR — permanent, employee-level system access and the right to publish their findings without the company's editorial control, aside from narrow redactions for security, legal, or commercial sensitivity. Second, coordinate safety standards among labs in democratic countries. Third, eventually extend to global coordination including China. The second and third steps, however, remain largely theoretical for now.

Within hours of publication, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman said publicly: "I agree with Dario — we need to pace the frontier." It was a rare moment of the top three labs pointing in the same direction.

## Antitrust is the catch

The problem is that in any industry, when the three dominant players sit down to coordinate their behavior, regulators tend to pay attention. Any formal agreement among Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google DeepMind would likely face scrutiny over whether it serves public safety or market consolidation. Bloomberg framed the effort as raising "antitrust questions," and warned it could function as a "regulatory wall" against smaller rivals.

Views diverge. On September 14, White House AI and crypto chief David Sacks argued that AI labs should build safe technology without antitrust help. By contrast, legal analysis — such as at Lawfare — argues that a well-designed antitrust framework can actually promote AI safety collaborations. In short, the central question has become where to draw the line between legitimate cooperation and collusion.

<div class="article-callout info">
The crux here is less about what has been agreed and more about which forms of cooperation are permissible under antitrust law — a question that remains unsettled. Sharing safety standards can read as consumer protection, or as a three-way cartel. Until a binding agreement emerges, this is a story to watch carefully.
</div>

## What it means and what's next

The confirmation shows two things at once: that the leading AI firms are beginning to translate their own worries about the pace of development into joint action beyond their rivalry — and that this joint action collides almost immediately with the old regulatory logic of market power and antitrust. With the US government skeptical of slowing development (the Trump administration has rejected a "slowdown"), the next thing to watch is whether the industry's attempt to build its own standards body turns into a binding framework, or stalls at the level of declarations under regulatory scrutiny.
