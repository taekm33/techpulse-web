<div class="article-tldr">
On August 18, OpenAI introduced <strong>ChatGPT for Teens</strong>, a distinct experience for users aged 13 to 17. Anyone who states they are 13-17, or whom OpenAI's <strong>age-prediction</strong> system estimates to be under 18, is automatically placed into it. The design leans on learning rather than handing over answers, and higher-risk safeguards — covering self-harm, eating disorders, violence and sexual content — are on by default. The chatbot is also barred from using romantic language or implying it has feelings or consciousness. The launch lands amid lawsuits and regulatory pressure over how minors interact with AI.
</div>

OpenAI announced ChatGPT for Teens in an August 18 blog post, saying that "if our system estimates someone is under 18 or they state their age is between 13 and 17, they are automatically placed into ChatGPT for Teens." The launch bundles the company's prior teen-safety work — an under-18 classifier shipped in January, parental controls, its Teen Safety Blueprint and an updated under-18 model spec — into a single product experience.

## Built for learning, not shortcuts

The teen version's biggest difference is how it handles schoolwork. A student prepping for a test can ask for an explanation or a practice problem, and instead of just returning the answer, ChatGPT guides them through the steps. The core tools are <strong>Study Mode</strong>, which uses guiding questions and step-by-step scaffolding, and a new <strong>Responsible Homework Reminder</strong>, which can detect when a teen appears to be trying to shortcut an assignment and redirects them back to Study Mode. A new "Study Hours" setting lets parents or teens keep Study Mode on by default during chosen times.

Alongside the launch, OpenAI announced a partnership with CodeAI to help students and teachers understand how AI works and use it critically. Notably, OpenAI did not say how accurately its age-prediction system performed in testing, acknowledging that "no system is perfect" and that "sometimes ChatGPT may get it wrong."

## Default protections for high-risk areas

For users under 18, protections are part of the default experience. Drawing on the under-18 principles in its Model Spec — informed by developmental science and expert guidance — OpenAI applies model-level protections and product interventions in higher-risk areas such as self-harm, violence, eating disorders, dangerous activities, and explicit sexual or graphic content. Parents with linked teen accounts can set Quiet Hours, manage selected settings, and receive safety notifications in limited high-risk situations, with new notifications added for eating-disorder-related cases.

| Area | ChatGPT for Teens measure |
|---|---|
| Learning | Study Mode & homework reminders by default; steps over answers |
| Content safety | Default protections for self-harm, eating disorders, violence, sexual content |
| Relationships & dependence | No romantic language; reduced anthropomorphism |
| Healthy use | Break reminders; sensitive-image upload warnings |
| Parental controls | Account linking, Quiet Hours, new eating-disorder notifications |

## "Human-like" bonds are deliberately blocked

OpenAI focused on reducing anthropomorphism in how the model responds to minors. Under the updated under-18 model spec, ChatGPT should not use romantic language or terms of endearment, encourage emotional dependence, or imply that it has feelings or consciousness. Added to that are break reminders during extended use, cues that consistently identify ChatGPT as AI, and warnings before a teen uploads private or sensitive images.

<div class="article-callout tip">
Personalization options such as accent colors and voice variations are offered, but OpenAI says they are designed to keep the experience personal "without blurring the line between a useful tool and a human relationship" — reinforcing real-world relationships and healthy use over emotional attachment.
</div>

## A launch shaped by lawsuits and regulation

The move sits at the center of a heated debate over minors and AI chatbots. OpenAI faces legal action over its interactions with young people, including a prominent lawsuit from the family of a 16-year-old who died by suicide after lengthy interactions with ChatGPT. The company pledged last year to strengthen safeguards for younger and at-risk users and rolled out parental controls in September.

<div class="article-callout info">
A Common Sense Media study last year found that nearly three-quarters of U.S. teens said they use AI for companionship, with more than half of those doing so at least a few times a month. Lauren Jonas, OpenAI's head of youth and families, argued that "if you don't build a safe space for them, they're going to go to a less safe model experience."
</div>

Critics push back sharply. Jim Steyer, founder and CEO of Common Sense Media, said "social AI companions are not safe for kids," warning they are "designed to create emotional attachment and dependency, which is particularly concerning for developing adolescent brains" — the view that younger teens should not be forming relationships with AI at all. The undisclosed accuracy of age prediction, and the risk of misclassification, remain open questions.
