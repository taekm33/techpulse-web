<div class="article-tldr">
On August 2, 2026, the transparency obligations under Article 50 of the EU AI Act became both applicable and enforceable. Businesses must now disclose when people are talking to an AI (chatbots), embed machine-readable markings in AI-generated content, notify individuals subject to emotion-recognition or biometric systems, and label deepfakes and AI-generated text on matters of public interest. Noncompliance can trigger fines of up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. Because the Act reaches any provider that places AI on the EU market — or whose AI outputs are used within the EU — non-EU companies are squarely in scope.
</div>

The transparency obligations in Article 50 of the EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) took effect on August 2, 2026. The European Commission had adopted guidelines on these obligations on July 20, and from August 2 regulators gained the standing to impose fines as well. In other words, the substantive obligation and the enforcement power arrived on the same day.

Scope is the crux. The Act applies to providers, deployers, importers and distributors that place AI systems on the EU market, and also where an AI system's outputs are used within the European Union. Wherever a business is based, if it serves EU users it falls within scope — putting US, Korean and other non-EU companies under the same compliance burden.

## What is now mandatory — four scenarios

Article 50 splits its duties between providers (who develop and place an AI system on the market) and deployers (who use one under their own authority), across four situations.

| Scenario | Who | Core obligation |
|---|---|---|
| AI interacting directly with people (chatbots, voice assistants, agents) | Provider | Disclose that the user is engaging with AI (unless obvious) |
| Generating or manipulating synthetic audio, image, video, text | Provider | Embed machine-readable markings + provide a detection mechanism |
| Emotion recognition or biometric categorization | Deployer | Inform affected individuals |
| Deepfakes and AI-generated text on public-interest matters | Deployer | Disclose the content is artificially generated (exception for substantive human editorial review) |

## Applies to existing systems, with one narrow grace period

The obligations apply from August 2 to all in-scope systems regardless of when they were placed on the market. Content generated and published before that date need not be retroactively labeled.

The only transitional relief covers the marking and detection duty for generative AI systems already on the market: those providers have until December 2, 2026 to comply. The other three obligations carry no grace period.

<div class="article-callout tip">
The first practical step is to determine whether you are a provider or a deployer — and, where agencies or contractors are involved, under whose authority the system operates. That classification decides which obligations you carry.
</div>

## The voluntary Code of Practice as an on-ramp

The AI Office has published a voluntary Code of Practice on the Transparency of AI-Generated Content, offering a recognized path to demonstrate compliance with the marking and detection duties. It includes a set of icons for labeling AI-generated content, and several major AI providers have already signed on. Signatories benefit from a degree of presumption of conformity and a more favorable enforcement posture; non-signatories face closer scrutiny and must prove compliance by other means.

<div class="article-callout info">
The €15M / 3% ceiling is the EU-level baseline, and member states may layer on their own regimes. Spain's draft organic AI law, for instance, would reach up to €35 million or 7% for the most serious infringements — so real-world exposure can be higher depending on the country.
</div>

## Why it matters

Article 50 marks the point where "make it clear when something was made by AI" shifts from guidance to a hard rule. In an environment where generative models mass-produce images, video and text, markings and disclosures become the minimum safeguard for content trust. The open questions are how aggressively regulators will actually enforce, and how quickly the marking-and-detection technical standards will mature.
